## Part 1: How payment fraud works

*Type: ARTICLE · About 3 min*

### One email, one payment, gone

Payment fraud doesn't need hacking skills. It needs one person to pay one invoice to the wrong account. Once money reaches a criminal's account it is often moved on within hours and is very hard to recover.

### The four common versions

#### 1. The "new bank details" supplier
An email that looks like it comes from a regular supplier says their bank account has changed. The next invoice should go to the new account. Sometimes the criminal has actually broken into the supplier's real mailbox, so the email comes from the **genuine address** and even continues a real conversation.

#### 2. The fake invoice
An invoice arrives for goods or services that look normal — cable, transport, customs clearing — from a company we might plausibly use. The amount is modest enough not to raise questions.

#### 3. The fake boss
A message appears to come from a Savikro manager or director: "I'm in a meeting, need an urgent transfer to close a deal, keep this confidential, I'll explain later."

#### 4. The customer "overpayment"
A customer claims to have overpaid and asks for a refund to a *different* account than the one they paid from.

### What they have in common
- A **change** to normal payment details or process
- **Urgency** ("today", "before the bank closes")
- **Secrecy** or a reason not to call ("lines are down", "I'm travelling")

If you see a payment request with any of these, stop and verify.

## Part 2: The call-back procedure, step by step

*Type: WALKTHROUGH · About 4 min*

### Use this every time bank details change or a payment request is unusual

This procedure comes directly from POL-EML-001, Section 4.4. It is **not optional**, however urgent the request looks.

#### Step 1 — Pause
Don't pay, don't update the bank details in any system, and don't reply to the message yet.

#### Step 2 — Find the number yourself
Look up the supplier's or customer's phone number from **company records** — a previous invoice, the supplier file or the contact list you already hold. **Never** use a phone number, email or link given in the request itself.

#### Step 3 — Call a person you know
Call the number and ask for your usual contact. Ask them to confirm:
- that they sent the request;
- the new account details, read back **by them**, not by you.

#### Step 4 — Record the check
Write down who you spoke to, when, and what they confirmed. Keep it with the supplier or customer file.

#### Step 5 — Get manager approval
A manager must approve the change **before** any payment is made to new details.

#### Step 6 — If anything doesn't add up, report it
If the supplier says they didn't send it, or you can't reach them, don't pay. Report it immediately: **Report an incident → Suspicious email / phishing**, and tell your manager.

> **Tip:** A genuine supplier will never be offended that you called to check. Most will be glad you did.

## Part 3: For managers: making verification normal

*Type: ARTICLE · About 2.5 min*

### Fraudsters count on pressure from the top

The "fake boss" scam only works if staff believe that questioning a manager's urgent request will get them into trouble. Managers can remove that belief.

### What managers should do
- **Never** request urgent or unusual payments by email or message alone. Say so openly to your team.
- **Thank** staff who call you to check a payment request — even when it really was you.
- **Never** ask anyone to skip the call-back procedure "just this once".
- Approve bank-detail changes only after seeing a record of the call-back check.
- Remember that LS Electric correspondence about commercial terms is **Restricted**. Criminals may pretend to be LS Electric because everyone knows we are their distributor.

### What staff should know
If a request seems to come from a manager and asks for secrecy or speed, call that manager on their **known** number. Verifying is always the right thing to do, and no one will be criticised for it.

### Question 1

A supplier emails from their genuine, familiar email address asking you to update their bank details. What should you do?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Update the details — the address is genuine, so it's safe
- [x] **B.** Follow the call-back procedure anyway
- [ ] **C.** Reply to the email to confirm
- [ ] **D.** Update the details and tell your manager afterwards

**Answer:** B  
**Why:** Criminals sometimes take over a supplier's real mailbox, so even a genuine address must be verified by phone using a number from company records.

### Question 2

Where should you get the phone number to verify a bank-detail change?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** From the email signature of the request
- [ ] **B.** From an internet search
- [x] **C.** From company records such as a previous invoice or the supplier file
- [ ] **D.** From the person who sent the request, by replying

**Answer:** C  
**Why:** Only a number already held in company records is trustworthy. Numbers in the request may lead straight to the criminal.

### Question 3

Which of these are common warning signs of payment fraud? (Select all that apply)

*Multiple choice — select all that apply*

- [x] **A.** A change to normal bank details
- [x] **B.** Pressure to pay today
- [x] **C.** A reason why you shouldn't phone to check
- [ ] **D.** An invoice matching a delivery you received and already verified

**Answer:** A, B, C  
**Why:** Change, urgency and discouraging verification are the three hallmarks of payment fraud.

### Question 4

During the call-back, how should the new account number be confirmed?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** You read the number from the email and ask them to say 'yes'
- [x] **B.** The supplier's contact reads the number to you
- [ ] **C.** It doesn't need confirming if they say they sent the email
- [ ] **D.** They email it again

**Answer:** B  
**Why:** Having the contact read the details to you makes sure the confirmation comes from them, not from the suspicious email.

### Question 5

Once the call-back is done, you can pay to the new details without anyone else's approval.

*True / False*

- [ ] **True**
- [x] **False**

**Answer:** False  
**Why:** POL-EML-001 requires a manager to approve the change before any payment is made to new details.

### Question 6

A message appears to come from a director: 'Urgent transfer needed to close a deal. I'm in meetings all day — keep this between us.' What should you do?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Make the transfer — directors have authority
- [ ] **B.** Reply asking for the account details again
- [x] **C.** Call the director on their known number to verify, and report it if they didn't send it
- [ ] **D.** Ignore it and say nothing

**Answer:** C  
**Why:** Urgency plus secrecy from 'the boss' is a classic scam. Verifying on a known number is always correct and managers are expected to welcome it.

### Question 7

A customer says they overpaid and asks for a refund to a different account from the one they paid from. What is the correct approach?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Refund to the new account as requested
- [x] **B.** Treat it as a bank-detail change: verify by phone using company records and get manager approval
- [ ] **C.** Refund half now and half later
- [ ] **D.** Ask them to confirm by email

**Answer:** B  
**Why:** A refund to a different account is a change of payment details and needs the same call-back check and approval.

### Question 8

As a manager, which behaviour best protects Savikro from 'fake boss' fraud?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Sending payment requests by WhatsApp to save time
- [x] **B.** Telling staff you will never request urgent payments by message alone, and thanking them for checking
- [ ] **C.** Allowing staff to skip checks for small amounts
- [ ] **D.** Keeping payment procedures confidential from staff

**Answer:** B  
**Why:** When staff know that verification is expected and welcomed, fake urgent requests from 'management' lose their power.
