## Part 1: "It's the director — I need this done now"

*Type: ARTICLE · About 3 min*

### Your name is the attacker's most useful tool

In executive impersonation (often called "CEO fraud"), a criminal pretends to be a senior manager and asks staff to do something urgent:

- "Pay this LS Electric invoice today — I'm in a meeting, don't call."
- "Send me the customer list for the board, quickly."
- "Buy gift cards for a client and send me the codes."

These messages work because staff are reluctant to question a director. They often come from look-alike email addresses, personal messaging accounts, or even cloned voice messages.

#### What management does about it
- Tell your teams, clearly and often: **you will never be criticised for calling to check a request from me.**
- Never give real instructions for payments or data through personal messaging apps.
- Follow the same approval process yourself — no shortcuts "because it's me".

## Part 2: When the attack is aimed at you

*Type: ARTICLE · About 3 min*

### Senior managers are high-value targets

Managers can approve payments, grant access and see Restricted information — so attackers target them directly ("whaling"):

- a fake "LS Electric" email about a change to distributor terms, with a link to "sign in and review";
- a fake bank or tax-office message about an urgent penalty;
- a "journalist" or "investor" asking about margins or the distributor agreement;
- a request at a trade event for "just a rough idea" of your pricing to a competitor's friend.

#### Your defences
- Treat unexpected links and attachments with suspicion, especially when they ask you to sign in.
- Verify through a contact you already hold — never through details in the message.
- The distributor agreement, commercial terms and margins are **Restricted**. Do not discuss them in public places or with outsiders.
- Report suspicious messages on the Platform. Your reports also warn your staff.

## Part 3: Approving safely, step by step

*Type: WALKTHROUGH · About 4 min*

### A payment or data request reaches you for approval. Here is the safe path.

#### Step 1 — Is it coming through the proper channel?
Approvals are given through company systems or company email. A request on personal WhatsApp or as a voice note is a warning sign.

#### Step 2 — Does it need two approvals?
Payments above the limit set by the Board need **two** management approvals. Do not approve alone because someone says it is urgent.

#### Step 3 — Verify anything unusual
Unusual amount, new bank details, new supplier, secrecy or urgency? Call the requester — or the supplier — using a number from company records.

#### Step 4 — Record your decision
Approve or reject in the system, so there is a record of who decided and why.

#### Step 5 — Back your staff
If a team member delayed something to check it with you, thank them. That habit is what stops fraud.

### Question 1

A staff member calls you to confirm a payment request that appeared to come from you. You did not send it. What is the right response?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Tell them they should have just paid it
- [x] **B.** Thank them for checking, stop the payment, and report it on the Platform
- [ ] **C.** Approve it anyway to avoid delaying the supplier
- [ ] **D.** Ignore it — it was probably a mistake

**Answer:** B  
**Why:** Checking was exactly right. Thank them so the habit continues, stop the payment, and report the impersonation attempt.

### Question 2

Which of these are warning signs of executive impersonation? (Select all that apply)

*Multiple choice — select all that apply*

- [x] **A.** An urgent payment request that says "don't call me"
- [x] **B.** Instructions sent from a personal messaging account
- [x] **C.** A request to buy gift cards and send the codes
- [ ] **D.** An approval request that arrives through the normal company system

**Answer:** A, B, C  
**Why:** Urgency, secrecy, unofficial channels and gift cards are classic signs. Requests through the normal system are expected.

### Question 3

Because a director has authority, a director's urgent payment instruction can skip the second approval.

*True / False*

- [ ] **True**
- [x] **False**

**Answer:** False  
**Why:** No one is exempt. Attackers impersonate directors precisely because staff expect them to skip controls.

### Question 4

You receive an email from "LS Electric" asking you to sign in through a link to review new distributor terms. What should you do?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Click the link and sign in — it is from our principal supplier
- [ ] **B.** Forward it to your team to review
- [x] **C.** Do not click; contact your known LS Electric contact directly, and report the email
- [ ] **D.** Reply asking whether the email is genuine

**Answer:** C  
**Why:** Unexpected sign-in links are a common phishing technique. Verify through a contact you already hold, and report the message.

### Question 5

At a trade event, a friend of a competitor asks for "just a rough idea" of Savikro's margins on LS Electric products. What should you do?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Give a range, since it is not an exact figure
- [x] **B.** Decline — margins and commercial terms are Restricted
- [ ] **C.** Share them, since everyone in the trade roughly knows
- [ ] **D.** Promise to email them later

**Answer:** B  
**Why:** Margins and distributor terms are Restricted. Even a rough figure helps a competitor and may breach the distributor agreement.
