## Part 1: Why Savikro is a target

*Type: ARTICLE · About 2.5 min*

### Why would criminals target us?

Savikro is the sole agent for LS Electric in Sri Lanka. That makes us useful to criminals in three ways:

- **Money moves through us.** We pay suppliers and receive payments from contractors, panel builders and shops. A criminal who convinces us to change a bank account number can steal a full payment in one email.
- **Our information has value.** Supplier pricing, dealer discounts and customer lists are worth money to competitors.
- **People know who we deal with.** Anyone can find out that we distribute LS Electric products, so it is easy for a scammer to pretend to be a supplier, a customer or a delivery company.

### The most common attacks

1. **Fake invoice or bank-change request.** "Our bank details have changed, please pay the attached invoice to this new account."
2. **Fake login page.** "Your mailbox is full — log in here to keep receiving email."
3. **Malicious attachment.** An "invoice" or "purchase order" that asks you to enable content or macros.
4. **Urgent request from the boss.** A message that appears to come from management asking for an urgent payment or gift cards, and asking you to keep it quiet.

The good news: almost every one of these attacks can be stopped by one habit — **pause, check, and report.**

## Part 2: Anatomy of a fake supplier email

*Type: WALKTHROUGH · About 4 min*

### Let's examine a fake email step by step

Below is an example of a scam email. The company "Lanka Cable Supplies" is fictional.

> **From:** Accounts – Lanka Cable Supplies <accounts@lankacable-supp1ies.com>
> **Subject:** URGENT: Updated bank details – payment due today
>
> Dear Customer,
>
> Due to an audit, our bank account has changed. Please make today's payment to the new account below. Payments to the old account will be rejected.
>
> Please confirm once transferred. Do not call our office as the lines are under maintenance.
>
> Regards, Accounts Department
> 📎 Invoice_8841.zip

#### Step 1 — Check the sender address
Look closely: **supp1ies** uses the number **1** instead of the letter **l**. Criminals register lookalike addresses that pass a quick glance.

#### Step 2 — Notice the pressure
"Payment due today" and "payments to the old account will be rejected" are designed to make you act before you think.

#### Step 3 — Spot the bank-detail change
Any change of bank details is the single biggest warning sign. Our policy (POL-EML-001) says it **must** be confirmed by phone using a number already in our records.

#### Step 4 — Notice attempts to stop you checking
"Do not call our office" is there to stop you from verifying. Genuine suppliers never discourage you from calling.

#### Step 5 — Check the attachment
A **.zip** file sent as an invoice is a common way to deliver malware. Do not open it.

#### Step 6 — Report it
Tap **Report an incident → Suspicious email / phishing**, attach a screenshot or the email file, and submit. Do not forward it to colleagues and do not reply.

### Question 1

A supplier emails to say their bank account has changed and asks you to pay today's invoice to the new account. What should you do?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Pay it, because the email came from the supplier's usual name
- [ ] **B.** Reply to the email asking them to confirm the change
- [x] **C.** Phone the supplier on a number already in company records to confirm, and get manager approval before paying
- [ ] **D.** Call the phone number given in the email

**Answer:** C  
**Why:** Bank-detail changes must be verified by phone using a number already held in company records. Replying or using contact details from the email could put you straight back in touch with the criminal.

### Question 2

Which sender address is most likely to be fake?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** sales@savikro.lk
- [x] **B.** accounts@lankacable-supp1ies.com
- [ ] **C.** An address you have emailed many times before
- [ ] **D.** All addresses are equally safe

**Answer:** B  
**Why:** Replacing a letter with a similar-looking number ('1' for 'l') is a common trick to create lookalike addresses.

### Question 3

Which of these are warning signs of a phishing message? (Select all that apply)

*Multiple choice — select all that apply*

- [x] **A.** It pressures you to act immediately
- [x] **B.** It asks you to keep the request secret
- [x] **C.** It asks you to log in through a link
- [ ] **D.** It is addressed to you by name

**Answer:** A, B, C  
**Why:** Urgency, secrecy and login links are classic warning signs. Being addressed by name does not make a message safe — criminals can easily find names.

### Question 4

The Administrator may sometimes ask you to email your password so they can fix your account.

*True / False*

- [ ] **True**
- [x] **False**

**Answer:** False  
**Why:** The Administrator will never ask for your password. Anyone asking for it — by email, message or phone — should be reported.

### Question 5

You receive a suspicious email. What is the best thing to do with it?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Forward it to colleagues to ask what they think
- [x] **B.** Report it through the platform's Report an incident form
- [ ] **C.** Delete it and say nothing
- [ ] **D.** Reply and ask the sender who they are

**Answer:** B  
**Why:** Reporting lets the Administrator warn others and block the sender. Forwarding spreads the risk; deleting silently means others may still fall for it.

### Question 6

You clicked a link and typed your password into a page that now looks suspicious. What should you do first?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Wait to see if anything bad happens
- [x] **B.** Change your password from a page you open yourself, report the incident, and phone the Administrator
- [ ] **C.** Log in again on the same page to check it worked
- [ ] **D.** Keep quiet so you don't get into trouble

**Answer:** B  
**Why:** Speed matters. Change the password, report it and call. Reporting a mistake in good faith never leads to disciplinary action.

### Question 7

An 'invoice' arrives as a .zip file from a company you don't normally deal with. What should you do?

*Single choice*

- [ ] **A.** Open it to see what the invoice is for
- [ ] **B.** Open it on a warehouse terminal instead of your own computer
- [x] **C.** Don't open it; report it as a suspicious email
- [ ] **D.** Save it to a USB drive to open later

**Answer:** C  
**Why:** Unexpected compressed attachments are a common way to deliver malware. Opening it on any company device puts the business at risk.

### Question 8

If an email turns out to be an authorised simulated phishing exercise, reporting it was still the correct thing to do.

*True / False*

- [x] **True**
- [ ] **False**

**Answer:** True  
**Why:** Reporting is always correct, whether the email is real or simulated. Reporters are counted as a positive result in awareness exercises.
